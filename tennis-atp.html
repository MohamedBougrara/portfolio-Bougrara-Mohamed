The 2024 ATP season in charts

This is my analysis of the 2024 season of the men’s professional tour, taken from my Observable notebook. It covers two themes, player profiles and match dynamics, in six charts, two of them interactive.

← Back to the project

The charts are drawn by the browser: they need JavaScript, which is disabled here. The commentary remains readable.

Key facts

  • 3,076matches
  • 443players
  • 69tournaments, plus the Davis Cup
  • 115minutes per match on average

Matches by surface.

I. Player profiles

First, the number of matches played on each surface.

Players mostly compete on hard courts. Hard courts hosted more matches than grass and clay combined.

However, the number of matches depends mechanically on how many tournaments are held on each surface. To check whether this dominance simply reflects the tournament calendar, let’s look at how the events are distributed.

The dominance of hard courts seen above is structural: most tournaments are played on this surface, 39 out of 69.

Conversely, the grass season is extremely short, with only 8 tournaments in the whole year, which explains the small number of matches played on grass.

The best-performing players

The players.

To avoid the statistical bias of players who played only a few matches, I kept the most active players on the tour: a player who wins his only match would show a 100% win rate.

The tenth most active player played 67 matches, and so did two other players: keeping all of them gives 12 players, rather than breaking a tie at random.

This chart sets their number of matches against their actual efficiency (the win rate). The goal is to see who is the best player across all surfaces.

Alexander Zverev played the most, with 90 matches, but Jannik Sinner is the most successful in terms of win rate, with 91% wins.

In practice, Sinner wins almost every time; behind him, Carlos Alcaraz loses one match in five, and most of the other players in this ranking about one in three.

How to use it

Pick two players and tick the surfaces (Clay, Grass, Hard) to see their respective win rates.

The dataset contains 443 players in total. Rather than an endless drop-down list, the selector has a built-in search: click and start typing a player’s name to find him (e.g. “Djokovic”).

Only players with more than 20 matches are listed.

Under each percentage, n is the number of matches played on that surface; hovering over a bar also shows the matches won.

Note: by default the comparison shows Jannik Sinner and Alexander Zverev, the most efficient and the most active player of the season.

This chart compares the win rate by player and by surface: it combines two variables, the player and the surface.

Why compare win rates?

A rate puts every player on the same scale: it compares efficiency rather than volume of play, and no player is favoured just by how many matches he played.

The trade-off: a rate computed over 10 matches is unstable, as each win moves it by 10 points.

That is why the selector only offers players with more than 20 matches, and why n stays on screen: Sinner’s 79% on clay rests on just 14 matches.

II. Match dynamics

The matches.

Why this chart?

To analyse match duration, the histogram is the natural choice. It shows the shape of the distribution and the most frequent durations.

What we see

  • The norm: of the 2,838 matches with a known duration, just over one in two (51.5%) lasts between 70 and 120 minutes. The median is 108 minutes (1 hour 48 minutes of play), the mean 115 minutes.
  • The skew: the distribution has a single peak but stretches out to the right (a long right tail). Very short matches (under 50 min) are rare, because points take time to play; matches of over 3 h are few, but they exist and pull the average up.
Match format

The length of a tennis match depends mechanically on its format (the number of sets needed to win). The filter above removes this bias, to show the real effect of the gap in level.

Missing data: the chart shows no results for clay in the best-of-5 format (Roland-Garros). The source data file has no match durations (minutes) for the 2024 edition of this tournament.

1. Clay

The drop in duration is not linear. Long, contested matches occur even when the gap exceeds 250 places.

One possible explanation: this slow surface may favour long rallies and let outsiders hold on, so quick wins are rarer. A note of caution, though: that last class holds only 22 matches.

2. Hard court

This curve follows the ranking logic most closely. Duration stays stable between players of similar level, then drops clearly beyond 250 places (77 best-of-3 matches).

One possible explanation: with a faster, truer bounce, a gap in skill may show up more directly in the score.

3. Grass

The trend is more irregular. The specific nature of grass-court play can produce tight sets even against lower-ranked players, stretching matches out.

The samples are small (12 to 55 best-of-3 matches per class), so part of the irregularity comes from the sample itself.

Summary of the analysis

Exploring the 2024 ATP season brought out two fundamental dynamics:

1. Efficiency pays

While Alexander Zverev played the most matches, it is Jannik Sinner who dominates through exceptional consistency (over 90% wins). The top of the rankings depends less on how much a player plays than on this ability to win almost every time.

2. The surface matters

In this data, ranking does not decide everything. On clay, lower-ranked players seem to resist more than on hard courts, where a wide ranking gap turns into a shorter match.

This finding calls for caution: on clay, the widest-gap class holds only 22 matches. The surface shapes how a match unfolds.
