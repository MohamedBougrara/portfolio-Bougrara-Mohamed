Six years of road accidents, queried in SQL

Where, when and for whom are the roads deadliest? I answered in SQL on the official 2019-2024 files, after checking that my death totals match the official figures. Every answer comes with the query that produced it.

Key findings

Three findings, 2019-2024

  • Outside built-up areas: 36.1% of accidents, but 67.5% of deaths.
  • Friday, 4 to 5 p.m.: the deadliest hour of the week (230 deaths in six years).
  • E-scooters: 564 casualties in 2019, 2,507 in 2024, 4.4 times as many.

These figures describe associations, not causes: the data does not say how many kilometres each road user travels.

Seven questions, seven queries

Each question fits in one SQL view on the star schema. The answer is on the left; on the right, the result, the table the query returns, and the query itself.

Question 1

How many road deaths, and what trend?

Each year is compared with the previous one using LAG(), and with 2019 using FIRST_VALUE(), over one named window.

    Injury accidents 2019-2024, metropolitan France · view answers.q1_deaths_by_year

    Question 2

    Who dies on the road?

    Comparing the share of deaths with the share of people involved corrects for sheer numbers: there are many cars, so many deaths in cars. Both shares come from SUM() OVER (), the column total without a subquery.

      Mode: the person’s own, not that of the vehicle at fault. A pedestrian is linked to the vehicle that hit them, so their role is tested before the vehicle category.

      Question 3

      In town or on open roads, where do people die?

      The fact table has one row per person: an accident is counted once thanks to count(DISTINCT num_acc), and the shares come from SUM() OVER ().

        Area as recorded in the accidents file (field agg).

        Question 4

        In which departments is an accident most often fatal?

        Lethality is the number of deaths per 100 casualties: it does not depend on the size of the department. RANK() ranks without breaking ties at random, NTILE(4) puts each department in a quarter.

          Departments ranked by lethality

          Question 5

          At what hour, on what day and in what light do most people die?

          One row per hour, one column per day: the pivot table is built in SQL, by conditional aggregation (FILTER (WHERE weekday = 1)), not in a spreadsheet. Lighting comes from its own dimension.

            Hour of the accident; day of the week from the date dimension (1 = Monday).

            Question 6

            E-scooters, a new source of casualties?

            Cycling casualties, roughly stable over the period, serve as a point of comparison. The running total of deaths comes from SUM() OVER w, the median age from percentile_cont(0.5).

              Motorised personal mobility devices (category 50 of the file), e-scooters included; riders and passengers only: pedestrians hit by one are not counted here.

              Question 7

              Does a fastened seat belt change the outcome?

                Drivers and passengers of cars. The restraint is read from each person’s three safety equipment fields.

                Model, checks and performance

                An answer is only as good as its joins. Here is the model behind the seven queries, the reconciliation with the official figures, the trap that would have skewed the figures, and what an index changes.

                The star schema

                A fact table in the centre, with one row per person involved in an injury accident, and five dimensions around it. The business rules (what a casualty is, what a mode of travel is, what metropolitan France covers) are written once, here.

                fact_victim
                • person_idPK
                • num_accaccident
                • date_keyFK
                • depFK
                • mode_keyFK
                • light_keyFK
                • road_keyFK
                • hour
                • in_built_up_area
                • killed0 / 1
                • hospitalised0 / 1
                • victim0 / 1

                Measures as 0 or 1: SUM() counts, AVG() gives a rate.

                Hover over or pick a dimension: its key lights up in the fact table.

                dim_date: one day per row, 2019 to 2024; the year and the day of the week (1 = Monday) derive from it.

                dim_mode: eight modes of travel, from pedestrian to heavy goods vehicle, grouping the vehicle categories of the file.

                dim_light: daylight, dawn, night with or without street lighting; the unknown code stays a row of its own.

                dim_department: names from INSEE’s official geographic code; is_metropolitan sets the scope of the official figures.

                dim_road: the category of the main road, a single one per accident (see the duplicate trap, just below).

                Data quality

                The duplicate trap

                Since 2023, the roads file describes every road at a junction. A direct join then multiplies each casualty by the number of roads. ROW_NUMBER() keeps the main road only, and a check makes sure one road per accident remains.

                  Files that change from one year to the next

                  The 24 files do not look alike. Each irregularity is handled at loading or cleaning time, then verified by checks after every load.

                  • 2019Departments “1” to “9” without a leading zeroPadded to two digits (lpad), as in the other years.
                  • 2020Windows line endingsStripped at loading, before \copy.
                  • 2022Accident key renamed Accident_IdLoaded into the same num_acc column.
                  • All“-1” means “not recorded”Becomes NULL: it never counts as a category.
                  • AllDecimal commas, no-break spacesReplaced before casting: replace(x, ' ', '') left the no-break space in place.
                  • 2023-2024Several roads per accidentA single one kept with ROW_NUMBER(), see above.
                  • Before 2021No person identifierSurrogate key set with ROW_NUMBER().
                  • AllMissing or implausible birth yearsAge left unknown rather than wrong.

                  Performance

                  What an index changes

                  EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS) runs the query and shows its plan. Without an index, PostgreSQL reads the whole fact table for a single department and a single year; with one, it goes straight to the rows it needs.

                  Times: median of seven runs, on a single machine. The order of magnitude is what matters, not the millisecond; the pages read do not depend on the machine.

                    Data: annual road injury accident databases, French Ministry of the Interior, data.gouv.fr, Open Licence. Department names: INSEE’s official geographic code. Queries run on PostgreSQL 16; charts drawn by the page, with no library.
